**Step B's format decision (added v4.1) — Parquet + Arrow, not CSV.** Store point-in-time datasets as Parquet and read them memory-mapped through Arrow's C++ API. The argument is not "faster ingest" — your `from_chars` CSV path is already fast — it is that the format changes what the pipeline *can* do: random access to history by timestamp is exactly what a point-in-time join needs, so leakage discipline becomes a property of the layout instead of a habit; the schema is explicit, which kills a class of silent column-order bugs; dictionary-encoded symbol columns are the string→integer transform a feature pipeline otherwise hand-rolls; and the **Arrow C Data Interface** hands the same buffers to PyTorch with no serialization. That last one is the AI-infra claim — the zero-copy *handoff*, not the ingest. Keep the CSV path and benchmark both: two ingest paths make the before/after free. Boundary to write down: zero-copy stops at any transform (non-native layout, string handling, a rolling window crossing a row-group boundary) — state where it stops rather than claiming it never does.

**Cost triage when the engine needs the hours (added v4.1).** Of the four anchor steps, three survive contact with the engine's schedule and one is capped:

- **H — keep.** It is the only *built* microstructure artifact in the portfolio, it is 1–2 weeks, and no amount of serving work teaches you what OFI and queue position mean. The engine cannibalizes its hot-path technique (you will have built faster paths by then) but not its domain content, which is the part a trading interviewer probes.
- **B — keep, scoped.** Bounded only because v4.1 scoped it to the dataset layer (point-in-time boundaries, embargo, Parquet, the zero-copy handoff). If it starts growing a feature store, a scheduler, or a catalog, it has left the plan.
- **D — keep.** Two to three days of statistics (purged CV, embargo, deflated Sharpe) that nothing else in this curriculum teaches, attached to the "null ensembles" claim already on the page.
- **C — literacy only, permanently.** One model, one training loop, one export, then stop. A half-built training pipeline is the one artifact in this spine that reads as dabbling, and the engine's decoder is a far stronger ML artifact than any small model trained here.
- **The pause rule is the time answer.** These are anchors: they pause when the engine needs the hours, by design. Cutting them to "free up time" trades a cheap, differentiated, non-substitutable item for hours that the engine's own gates will absorb anyway.

**Post-engine stretch tier (v4.2) — the AI-infra gap closers.** Gated on the engine having shipped (phase 6 green): none of it competes with the spine, and all of it answers the two standard objections to this portfolio — *"the model is small"* and *"there is no distributed or production story."* Tier A is ~2 weeks total and is the part actually worth committing to; Tier B is pick-by-remaining-hours.

**Tier A — cheap, high density.**

1. **Ring all-reduce + the alpha–beta crossover (~4–6 days).** Implement ring all-reduce over TCP — the general N-rank form, run as N=2 across the desktop and laptop and N=4 as local processes — then measure latency and bandwidth against message size and fit the latency-plus-bandwidth cost model. Plot the crossover: the message size above which tensor parallelism pays, per interconnect (1GbE, 2.5GbE, loopback, and with the rented node in Tier B, PCIe and NVLink). This is the cheap distributed artifact the master's non-goal line names, and it is a better one than a replicated log for this door: **the artifact is the measurement that decides when TP is worth it**, and it needs no special hardware. Drills: why ring and not tree; name the two terms of the cost model and which dominates at which size; why TP needs two all-reduces per layer.
2. **Decoder architecture breadth — RoPE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, GQA (~3–5 days).** Extend the from-scratch decoder past GPT-2 to a Llama-shaped config, behind the *same* token-for-token gate against a reference running the same tiny checkpoint. **GQA is why this earns the days**: grouped-query attention is the KV-cache reduction technique every serving stack uses, and owning it converts the KV-budget arithmetic from a GPT-2 curiosity into the standard story (KV per token drops by the query-to-KV-head ratio). This closes "GPT-2 is small" with real technical content at a fraction of the cost of serving an actual 7B — and it needs no GPU, because correctness runs on a tiny config and the interesting part is architecture-level.
3. **Snapshot-published metrics + the SLO write-up (~3–4 days).** A sub-microsecond path cannot be scraped, so counters are published from a seqlock-protected snapshot the hot path writes and the exporter reads — the same mechanism phase 1 already assigns the McKenney reading for, now with a second consumer. Then the policy half: TTFT and TPOT SLOs at a stated offered rate, an error budget each, and what burns them. **No dashboards, no operator, no OpenTelemetry**; the claim is the publication mechanism and the policy, not the tooling.

**Tier B — the big items, in order.**

4. **Decoder quantization, end to end (1–2 weeks).** A5 measures one quantized kernel; this measures what quantization does to *the serving path* — throughput gain against accuracy cost (perplexity plus token-agreement rate versus the FP32 gate), and where the error lands. Gated on A5, and cheaper than it looks because the correctness harness already exists.
5. **Speculative decoding (~1 week).** Draft (GPT-2) plus target, in your own decoder. The finding is the interesting half: it is a batch-1 latency technique, so its gain collapses exactly as continuous batching's throughput rises — two techniques pulling the same knob in opposite directions, measured on one system.
6. **Tensor parallelism on your own decoder, one rented node (2–3 weeks).** Shard attention heads and MLP columns, place the all-reduces, shard the KV cache, re-run the token-for-token gate across rank counts. Highest-ceiling claim available without a cluster, and tractable precisely because item 1 built the collective's moving parts by hand first.

**Marginal — only if everything above ships:** a disaggregated prefill/decode study (3–5 days) — model the KV-transfer cost, validate the model against your measured TTFT/TPOT, report the crossover. Honest because it is a model validated against a real system rather than a deployed one.

**Skipped, with reasons (so they do not creep back).** *Kubernetes* — a Dockerfile and one manifest are two hours and earn no claim; cluster operations is a different job than this portfolio targets. *Serving a real 7B* — downloading a checkpoint and putting it behind your gateway adds no code the decoder does not already have, and item 2 closes the same vocabulary gap with actual engineering. *Multi-node GPU across your own two machines* — impossible rather than merely hard: ROCm and CUDA GPUs cannot join one collective, and consumer Ethernet adds ~4–6 ms of pure latency per token across 64 collectives — which is exactly why item 1's crossover measurement exists. *Grafana/OpenTelemetry/operators* — tooling, not findings.

**Hardware (cloud route, your choice):** CUDA runs only on NVIDIA — the RX 9070 XT can't, the laptop's RTX 3060 can, but the chosen path is cloud: Colab's free tier (T4) or ~$1/hr instances cover the entire CUDA workload, kernels bench and decoder included — same rent-the-hardware pattern as the NUMA cloud day in [`12-phase-2-microarch.md`](12-phase-2-microarch.md). Use real `nvcc` + Nsight in the cloud, never a translation layer (ZLUDA / ROCm-on-consumer): the profiling toolchain is part of what you're learning, and the resume currency is *CUDA* specifically.

**Retired in v4 — the optional API-vs-ONNX backend comparison.** Its gate (old step E) no longer exists, and its content is superseded by the phase-6 writeup on per-provider determinism. If it ever returns, it returns as a fresh decision with its own gate, not as drift back into this plan.

**The one form that return has been offered in (v4.1, not adopted):** a TensorRT crossover study — engine build, FP16/INT8 calibration, and a batch-size sweep against the CPU path to find where a GPU actually wins. It is a real deployment skill with a real lesson (a sub-microsecond decision runs on CPU, so moving it to a GPU *costs* latency at batch 1, and the win only appears as an intersection on the curve). It stays unadopted because it re-introduces the second backend that §cut-order item 2 removes *first*, and because the phase-6 sweep plus the A4 roofline already answer the "CPU vs. GPU at batch=1" drill analytically. Re-open it only as a post-shipping decision, with the crossover framing — never as a speedup claim, which at batch 1 would be false.

**Two rules (non-negotiable):**

1. **Models are plumbing, not alpha.** Every model is tiny and honest; all claims are about the pipeline and its measurement, never model performance. Scripted probe answer: "my work is the serving and performance layer; the model is a placeholder — that's a deliberate boundary, not a gap."
2. **Bracket rule applies.** No AI bullet ships without its measurement; the ONNX stub reaches the resume only when replay-pinned determinism is demonstrated.

**Deliberately skipped (know the vocabulary, can't claim the build):** distributed training / NCCL, RAG/vector DBs, MLOps-at-scale, speculative decoding. (KV caches left this list when step G was specified; **continuous batching left it in v4** — it is now the centerpiece, [`16-phase-6-scheduler.md`](16-phase-6-scheduler.md). Everything else here stays vocabulary-only.) If probed: the boundary answer above. **Resurrected by the AI extension spine** (previously cut in this spec, now covered): COD 6.6 + Appendix C and 6.7 (slotted into step A), COD 3.5/3.6–3.7 upgraded to real reads (see thrive layer), DDIA ch. 10–11 figures skim. Still skipped: CiA ch. 9–10 (one thread-pool paragraph only, see thrive layer), OSTEP persistence half, Top-Down ch. 7–8.

**End-state narrative:** data → features → training → export → serving → measurement — one engine carrying kernels, decoder, gateway, and scheduler, with the Orderbook and Backtester as the quant anchors. Small bonus: note "HIP-portable kernel design" in the CUDA writeup (knowing where the portability boundary sits is itself a signal).

### AI Extension Spine: Learning Curriculum (concepts → resources → interview drills)

Keyed to the pipeline steps so each concept is applied within days of learning it. Your systems background is a shortcut throughout: GPU memory hierarchy, tail latency, and throughput-vs-latency are the same physics as Drepper/COD/Gil Tene, re-skinned — that's the interview edge; the drills below are what "thriving" looks like.

**Step H — feature layer (Orderbook).** *Concepts:* order-flow imbalance, microprice, queue-position estimation; why these predict short-horizon prices (adverse selection, informed flow). *Resources:* Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov, *The Price Impact of Order Book Events* (2014 — short paper, the OFI original); Harris, *Trading and Exchanges*, queue/limit-order chapters (skim). *Drill:* "why does order imbalance predict short-horizon moves?"

**Step B — dataset pipeline (Backtester).** *Concepts:* point-in-time correctness; label leakage / lookahead bias (the #1 probed topic); time-series splits — never shuffle; purged CV + embargo periods; class imbalance; rolling z-scores. *Resources:* López de Prado, *Advances in Financial Machine Learning*, ch. 3–7 only (dense but the reference); scikit-learn docs for mechanics. *Drills:* "why does random k-fold fail on time series?" "where does leakage hide in feature engineering?"

**Step C — training literacy (Backtester, Python).** *Concepts:* the floor — linear/logistic regression, gradient boosting (LightGBM/XGBoost), loss functions, overfitting vs. regularization, bias-variance; PyTorch floor — tensors, autograd, one training loop (learn to *talk to* ML engineers, not become one); ONNX as exchange format. *Resources:* PyTorch *60-Minute Blitz* (free, an evening); LightGBM/XGBoost docs; `onnx`/`onnxruntime` docs. **Skip ML textbooks** — a time sink for plumbing-level literacy. *Drills:* "why GBM over a neural net here?" "what's actually inside an ONNX file?"

**Steps E+F — retired, absorbed into the engine.** E's build (ONNX Runtime on the hot path) becomes the optional second backend; E's learning and F's sweep move into [phase 6](16-phase-6-scheduler.md). *What survives as material:* **numeric determinism** — FP non-associativity, why CPU vs. GPU inference can differ bitwise, and the **ONNX Runtime determinism and execution-provider documentation** (determinism is not a global switch but a per-provider property — kernel selection, reduction order, and provider fallbacks all vary; the reading is "which knob pins which provider, and where the guarantee stops"). It is the source for phase 6's required writeup. *Also surviving:* ONNX Runtime C++ API docs (only if the second backend ships), TensorRT docs skim (why INT8/FP16 buys throughput — the quantization reading behind A5). *Drills survive verbatim:* "CPU vs. GPU inference at batch=1 — walk the tradeoff" (answered from your own phase-6 sweep), "what is quantization and what does it cost?", "why can inference be non-deterministic?"

**Step A — CUDA (Backtester + the inference engine's kernels module, the big one).** Five sub-steps. Every read below unlocks the build under it, and A5 comes last because quantization is the only part that needs the precision ladder. The kernels bench lives in the **inference engine repo**, not inside the Backtester — a reviewer opening the Backtester should find a simulation engine, and a reviewer opening the engine should find measured kernel tables next to the decoder whose bandwidth claim they explain. The GPU Monte Carlo stays in the Backtester, where its CPU counterpart and replay harness already are.

**A1. 📖 *GPU architecture.* COD 6.6 (GPU intro) + Appendix C skim, then 6.7 (DSAs/TPU) skim** — SIMT, warps, thread blocks, and the GPU memory hierarchy (registers → shared → L2 → HBM) at textbook altitude before touching code; this is also the accelerator-landscape vocabulary (TPU as archetypal DSA) for AI-chip company interviews. Drepper §1–3 and COD ch. 5, re-skinned.
🔨 **Build:** NVIDIA DLI free intro course (few evenings, hands-on).
✅ **Verify:** why is a GPU bandwidth-rich but latency-poor?

**A2. 📖 *Programming model and coalescing.* CUDA C Programming Guide** as desk reference — kernel launches, grid/block/thread geometry, `__global__`, memory **coalescing** — plus *streams and async*: `cudaMemcpyAsync`, overlapping H2D/compute/D2H, directly analogous to your SPSC pipelining.
🔨 **Build:** *GPU Monte Carlo in the Backtester.* Path generation on GPU, coalesced, seeded CPU↔GPU bit-identical replay, profiled with Nsight Systems on the cloud T4. Do this sub-step first among the CUDA work: it is the "make CUDA run at all" step and it reuses your already-shipped CPU Monte Carlo, so the correctness story is a replay comparison rather than a new invariant. **AI hook preserved:** per-path seeding and the deterministic partition scheme carry over unchanged from the CPU version; the CPU↔GPU equivalence test is the same harness you use for thread-count invariance.
✅ **Verify:** your CPU↔GPU seeded replay story — same path hashes, same terminal equities, across the device boundary.

**A3. 📖 *Tiling and shared memory.* Kirk & Hwu, *Programming Massively Parallel Processors* (4th ed., ch. 1–4)** — thread/block geometry, memory coalescing, shared memory, tiling, and bank conflicts.
🔨 **Build:** *the GEMM ladder, part 1.* Naive → coalesced → shared-memory tiled. Measure each rung as you add it; the ladder table is the artifact, and a rung that buys nothing is a finding worth keeping.
🎯 **Drill:** "walk me through coalescing."

**A4. 📖 *Occupancy and roofline.* Kirk & Hwu ch. 5–6** — occupancy, performance limits, and the matrix-multiplication case study — plus the **Nsight Compute** tutorials (the kernel-level profiler: occupancy achieved, memory throughput, stall reasons) and the roofline model in its primary source — **Williams, Waterman & Patterson, *Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model* (2009)**, short and citable, and the origin of the arithmetic-intensity framing you will be asked to reproduce on a whiteboard.
🔨 **Build:** *the GEMM ladder, part 2.* Register-blocked kernel, occupancy sweep, and the **roofline figure** — achieved FLOPs and achieved DRAM bandwidth plotted against the machine's ceilings. Document where the ladder stops scaling and what the limiter is. Profiling is part of the build, not a postscript: every rung's number gets Nsight Compute output linked to it.
✅ **Verify:** roofline a kernel out loud; explain how occupancy interacts with shared memory; say which rung hit which ceiling and why.

**A5. 📖 *Precision and quantization.* COD 3.5 (IEEE-754) as a real read** — you cannot answer "what does quantization cost" without knowing what a mantissa is — **plus quantization material: TensorRT's quantization docs upgraded from skim to read, and one INT8 GEMM reference.**
🔨 **Build:** *one fused or quantized kernel.* INT8 dequant-GEMM or fused attention — pick one, not both. Measure throughput gain and accuracy cost against the FP32 baseline, and write both numbers down.
🎯 **Drill:** "what is quantization and what does it cost?", answered from your own accuracy delta, not from a blog.

**Not here: CUDA Graphs.** The Monte Carlo path generator is one wide kernel, so launch overhead is invisible against its runtime and graph capture is a null result at A2. The technique is real one workload away — the decoder's decode iteration is dozens of small kernels — and it is built as [phase 6](16-phase-6-scheduler.md)'s stretch rung. The general rule this establishes: a technique is not a plan item until the workload makes it pay, and knowing which workload that is *is* the learning.

*RISC-V note:* if the accelerator-company path (Tenstorrent/Openchip-style) firms up, skim COD's RISC-V "Real Stuff" sections — "RISC-V vs. x86" is a known probe.

**Step G — decoder (transformer internals, the engine's backend, ~1–1.5 weeks).** *Gate:* after A (the roofline and bandwidth reasoning are what make decode legible). The old E/F gate is retired. This step converts the know-only LM vocabulary into a measured artifact **and** becomes the model backend the scheduler batches — but the decoder is still built and verified single-stream first, and the cut list below is what keeps it from becoming a serving engine itself.

*Cut list, fixed in advance:* no tokenizer beyond a naive BPE over a dumped vocab; no HTTP server, no OpenAI-compatible API, no transport of any kind (the gateway owns the ingress path); **no batching inside the decoder** — it runs a batch handed to it by phase 6, it does not schedule one (that line changed in v4; previously batching was cut entirely); no quantization of the decoder; no GPU path in v1; nothing larger than GPT-2 124M.

*Interface obligation (added v4):* the KV cache is built behind an allocator interface even while the implementation is contiguous, and the decoder implements the model-backend interface defined in phase 1. Both seams exist so phase 6 can add paging and scheduling without rewriting attention. See [`04-inference-engine-pivot.md`](04-inference-engine-pivot.md) §6.

*Concepts:* scaled dot-product attention, multi-head attention, causal masking, layernorm, the residual stream, prefill vs. decode, the KV cache and what it costs in memory, why decode is bandwidth-bound and prefill is compute-bound, sampling (greedy, temperature, top-k), TTFT vs. inter-token latency.

*Resources:* Jay Alammar, *The Illustrated Transformer* (~1 hr, free — attention mechanics without the math overhead) → Karpathy, *Let's build GPT: from scratch, in code, spelled out* (~2 hrs — mechanism only, the Python is incidental; this is the one ML video worth the time) → llama.cpp's architecture docs and a read of its KV-cache and sampling source as a **C++ reference implementation** — reading a working C++ implementation of the thing you are about to write is the highest-leverage hour in this step → *Attention Is All You Need*, skim for vocabulary only. The bandwidth reasoning comes from A4 and the precision ladder from A5; nothing new is needed for either.

*Build, in this order:* (1) a one-file Python weight-dump script — GPT-2 124M weights and vocab to flat binaries; Python stops there. (2) Naive decoder: embeddings → single-head attention → MLP → layernorm → logits → greedy decode, CPU, fp32. (3) **Correctness gate before any optimization:** greedy output must match the reference token-for-token on a fixed prompt for the first N tokens. **The reference must run the same weights and the same tokenizer as your decoder** — GPT-2 124M weights dumped from HF compared against a llama.cpp GGUF of a different model family is not a gate, it is a guaranteed mismatch. Pick the pair deliberately (HF `transformers` running the same checkpoint is the natural reference; a llama.cpp GGUF of the *same* GPT-2 model works too) and write down which one, so the claim "verified token-for-token against llama.cpp" is true as stated rather than approximately true. This is your bit-identical-replay habit applied to a new domain, and it is the single most credible thing about the artifact — most from-scratch decoders in portfolios have no correctness claim at all. (4) KV cache, re-run the correctness gate, then re-benchmark. (5) Multi-head, then sampling if cheap. (6) Measure: tok/s single-stream; TTFT at 512/1024/2048 context; achieved DRAM bandwidth during decode against the A4 roofline number; KV-cache footprint vs. context length. (7) *Stretch only, after everything above ships:* a static batch-size sweep for curiosity (tok/s rises, per-token latency rises, and the bandwidth ceiling is the explanation) — the real sweep is phase 6's, over continuous batching — then a GPU path. Everything in (6) is single-stream and stays that way; concurrency belongs to the scheduler, and keeping the decoder's numbers clean of it is what makes the token-for-token gate meaningful.

*Drills:* "walk me through attention and the KV cache"; "why is decode bandwidth-bound and prefill compute-bound?" (answer from your own achieved-bandwidth figure); "what does the KV cache cost at 2k context and why does that cap batch size?"; "how did you verify your implementation?" — token-for-token against llama.cpp.

*Resume line shape:* "From-scratch GPT-2-class decoder in C++17 (multi-head attention, KV cache, greedy decode): [X] tok/s single-stream, [N] ms TTFT at 2k context; output verified token-for-token against llama.cpp."

*Architecture breadth (post-engine, Tier A item 2 above):* the decoder ships GPT-2-shaped. Extending it to a Llama-shaped config — RoPE, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and **grouped-query attention** — is what turns the KV-cache arithmetic into the standard story instead of a small-model curiosity, and it is the cheapest answer to "GPT-2 is small" that involves writing code rather than renting hardware.

**Step D — evaluation rigor (Backtester).** *Concepts:* walk-forward (known); purged/embargoed CV applied; **multiple testing and the deflated Sharpe ratio** (your null ensembles were always a homemade version of this); information coefficient. *Resources:* Bailey & López de Prado, *The Deflated Sharpe Ratio* (short paper, very readable); ALFM ch. 11–12 skim. *Drills:* "how many backtests did you run before finding this?" "why does Sharpe overstate edge after selection?"

**Cross-cutting "thrive" layer:** FP precision ladder (FP32/TF32/FP16/INT8 — what quantization buys and costs; **COD 3.5 is now a real read**, not a skim — you can't answer "what does quantization cost" without knowing what a mantissa is; 3.6–3.7 SIMD likewise read properly — the CPU-side ancestor of GPU vector lanes); GPU-vs-CPU physics (bandwidth vs. latency, why batch amortizes); tail latency under serving load (Gil Tene — done); batch vs. stream vocabulary (DDIA ch. 10–11 figures-only skim, ~1 hour, ten Anki cards — step B/H are batch and streaming respectively); **thread pools, one paragraph** — what they are, why serving frameworks use them, and how your SPSC-spinning model differs (the contrast is the interview answer); the know-only list (NCCL/all-reduce vocabulary, distributed training concepts, MLOps). Every one is answered with either a measurement from your build or the boundary script — that pairing is the signal.

**Sequencing note (v4):** the engine path is **Phase 0 → A → G → Phases 1–5 → Phase 6**, so the kernels ladder and the decoder exist before the gateway and scheduler need them. The anchor path is H→B→C→D on Orderbook and Backtester, landing whenever their own gates are green.
